Mars-centred view, to scale, with Mars's north pole up. Mars turns once in 24 h 37 min 23 s about its real pole, and the grid on it is every 30° of latitude and longitude; the arrowheads on its equator point the way the ground moves. The amber line through Mars is the stretch of its orbit round the Sun that it is on, 27 days behind it and 27 ahead. Phobos and Deimos follow precessing ellipses built from JPL's mean elements: Phobos's orbit is tilted 1.08° to Mars's equator and that tilt swings round once in 2.26 years; its distance from Mars's centre varies between 9,234 and 9,518 km over each orbit. Deimos's orbit swings round a plane 0.9° off the equator once in 56 years, so its tilt to the equator is anywhere from 0.9° to 2.7°. These give the shape and motion of the system, not a forecast of where along its orbit a moon will be on a given date in the 2080s. The Sun is at its true direction and distance. The three views hold different things still: the stars, the ground of Mars, or Phobos. The Phobos skyhook is a tether built on Phobos, lying along the line from Mars's centre through Phobos's, its own swinging left aside: each point keeps its distance from Phobos and is carried round Mars as that line turns. Its altitudes are given with Phobos at its mean distance, and the whole tether rises and falls 142 km each orbit. A ship is let go with the position and velocity its release point has at that instant. Its path is integrated (RK4) under Mars's gravity, the extra pull of Mars's equatorial bulge, and the Sun, until it comes down to 125 km (taken as the top of the air), has gone once round its orbit, or leaves Mars's sphere of influence. In a turning view the path is the ship's track against whatever the view holds still. The Release time slider spans one orbit of Phobos; the Flight slider moves the whole scene to that time after release. The other facilities and flights from one to another are added in later steps.
