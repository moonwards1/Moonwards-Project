Flight

Every facility follows the clock: the date and the release time decide where the departure skyhook's release point is, and how it is moving, when it lets the ship go. A ship bound for the other skyhook can be caught anywhere along that tether, by a catcher that moves along it and absorbs up to 800 m/s of relative motion.

Mars-centred view, to scale, with Mars's north pole up. Mars turns once in 24 h 37 min 23 s about its real pole, and the grid on it is every 30° of latitude and longitude; the arrowheads on its equator point the way the ground moves. The amber line through Mars is the stretch of its orbit round the Sun that it is on, 27 days behind it and 27 ahead. Phobos and Deimos follow precessing ellipses built from JPL's mean elements: Phobos's orbit is tilted 1.08° to Mars's equator and that tilt swings round once in 2.26 years; its distance from Mars's centre varies between 9,234 and 9,518 km over each orbit. Deimos's orbit swings round a plane 0.9° off the equator once in 56 years, so its tilt to the equator is anywhere from 0.9° to 2.7°. These give the shape and motion of the system, not a forecast of where along its orbit a moon will be on a given date in the 2080s. The Sun is at its true direction and distance. The three views hold different things still: the Sun, with the ecliptic level, the ground of Mars, or Phobos. The Phobos skyhook is a tether built on Phobos, lying along the line from Mars's centre through Phobos's, its own swinging left aside: each point keeps its distance from Phobos and is carried round Mars as that line turns. Its altitudes are given with Phobos at its mean distance, and the whole tether rises and falls 142 km each orbit. A ship is let go by the skyhook chosen as From on the Flight card, with the position and velocity its release point has at that instant. Bound for the other skyhook, it is judged at its closest pass to that tether: caught if it comes within 1 km of the tether at no more than 800 m/s relative to it. Its path is integrated (RK4) under Mars's gravity, the extra pull of Mars's equatorial bulge, and the Sun, until it comes down to 125 km (taken as the top of the air), has gone once round its orbit, or leaves Mars's sphere of influence. In a turning view the path is the ship's track against whatever the view holds still. The Release time slider spans one orbit of the skyhook the ship leaves; the Flight slider moves the whole scene to that time after release. The hanging skyhook is a tether held upright on a circular orbit of its own, turning at the rate of a free orbit at its centre of mass; Mars's equatorial bulge turns that orbit's plane, and at the sun-synchronous tilt it turns once a Mars year, keeping pace with the Sun (which, on Mars's eccentric orbit, runs up to 11° ahead of or behind that steady pace). Its tether passes through the plane of Phobos's orbit twice an orbit; the clearance of a pass is the least distance between the two tethers around it. Moving ballast along the tether changes how fast the skyhook goes round without changing its angular momentum, so a sidestep — ballast out, held, and home again — slips it round its orbit and leaves the orbit as it was, with nothing for thrusters to make up. The tether's masses are the Hanging skyhooks calculator's model with best lunar silica at a safety factor of 3; the strain on the tether while the ballast is away from home is not checked. The space elevator is one tether from an anchor off the equator, in that anchor's meridian plane and turning with Mars: it bows toward the equatorial plane and runs out, a few hundred km off it, to a counterweight beyond areostationary orbit. Its cable is sized to its working strength everywhere, carrying a track (1.6 kg/m at the anchor, thinning to 0.4 kg/m) and no cargo; the ground's height at the anchor is left out. A tether built on Phobos sweeps farther from the equatorial plane the farther out it reaches, so its top is held short of where it would come within 50 km of the elevator's tether. Deimos crosses the elevator's meridian every 131 h; where it would pass within 50 km of the tether, the tether's slowest north-south swing (about a day long) is built up to 60 km at Deimos's distance by holding climbers on a slanted stretch low down for half of each swing, timed so the tether stands at the far end of its swing as Deimos crosses, and then brought to rest the same way. The drawn tether shows that swing, and the westward lean that a steady flow of cargo up it gives it. Left out of the elevator: the pull of the Sun and the moons on the tether, Mars's gravity beyond its equatorial bulge, the east-west sway that changes in the cargo flow cause, how the swing changes the tension, and the cable's stretch. The tether with its cargo aboard is worked out in full, in three dimensions, its counterweight held at its design distance. The hanging skyhook is timed against the elevator's tether as against Phobos's: Mars carries the elevator's tether through the plane of the skyhook's orbit twice a Mars day, and a pass is too near where the hanging tether is then close to it round its orbit. A whole number of orbits to one Mars day or two keeps the skyhook's place at those passes fixed; no period is in step with both that and Phobos. The mass driver, and flights to and from the elevator, are added in later steps.
